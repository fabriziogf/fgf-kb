# Communicating decisions to leadership

One job: give leadership enough confidence, clarity, and traceability to make an informed
endorsement — quickly. The structure is the same whether it's a five-minute verbal
briefing or a written summary.

> **Lead with the outcome, not the process.** The most common failure is opening with
> "we ran a scoring exercise, considered four factors, talked to all the teams, and
> here's what we learned." Leadership wants: "we're investing the next six weeks in X and
> Y because of Z. We're deferring A and B. Here's the risk we're accepting." Front-load
> the decision; the process goes in an appendix or an offer to walk through it.

## The template

| Section | What to include |
|---|---|
| **Headline** (1 sentence) | What you're investing in and the single most important reason. No preamble, no "after careful consideration" |
| **What you are not doing** (1 sentence per item) | Name each deferred request explicitly — don't bury them. For each: the request, why deferred, and what changes it next cycle |
| **Risk you are accepting** (1–2 sentences) | The downside of your trade-off, named. How you'll monitor it and what triggers re-evaluation |
| **How you will revisit** (1 sentence) | The conditions under which the decision changes. Signals this is a decision for *this cycle*, not a permanent one |

## Calibrating by audience

- **VP / SVP** — headline and risk in two or three sentences. Doesn't need the scoring rationale unless they ask. Listening for: do you have conviction, have you thought about the downside, will the teams execute? Bring a written summary they can forward.
- **Director / Senior Director** — wants the rationale as well. Will likely ask why specific requests were deferred; be ready with one sentence and the specific criterion per deferral. Expect pushback on at least one.
- **Engineering lead** — wants delivery commitments and scope assumptions, not strategic rationale. Bring capacity estimates and risk flags.

## What to avoid

- **Hedging** — "we think this might be right, but…" signals you aren't confident. If you genuinely aren't, say why specifically and what would change it. Don't hedge vaguely.
- **Burying the trade-off** — mentioning deferrals as an afterthought after three minutes on the wins.
- **Over-explaining process before the conclusion** — leadership doesn't need the four scoring dimensions before knowing what you decided.
- **Asking for permission when you mean alignment** — "do you think this is the right call?" is a different question from "does this have your support?" Know which you're asking.
- **Presenting the decision as permanent** — every prioritization decision is for this cycle. Name what would trigger a revision.

## The written summary

After any verbal alignment, send a written summary within 24 hours. Four purposes: it
forces you to crystallize your thinking, creates a shared reference that prevents
revisionism ("I thought we agreed to X"), gives leadership something forwardable, and
protects you if priorities shift and someone claims the decision was made without full
information.

Half a page with the four template components is enough. What it must be is **specific
and unambiguous**.
